Gravity Pulls

Faking Art, Again

I’ve told you about the story of how I taught my friend to fake piano skills (check this older blog post out if you don’t know what I’m talking about.) As a musician, something about the mere attempt to reduce music making to something so trivial doesn’t quite sit right with me, but I also find the idea of determining what gives art structure and pairing it with randomness to create new art pretty fascinating. Yes, I will be faking art… again

Thinking about structure in music, the gravity analogy came to mind—at least the way I understand it, the idea that notes and harmonies tend to move in accordance with the force of “musical pull”. It is an incredibly general statement, but it is true that si seems to be strongly attracted to do, or that fa seems to want to go down to mi. For this project, I wanted to make that pull literal. If a melody is a thing that wanders around and eventually falls back home, then the most honest instrument for it is a thing that wanders around and eventually falls back home: a ball with a weight in it.

The Vision

Just a sphere, 9 cm in diameter, with a motion sensor sealed inside and additional weight on one of the poles, and a round plywood arena for it to roll in. Ball pushed → rolls → bumps into the wall a few times → wobbles → settles with the heavy pole down. A second later, a Raspberry Pi plays a short piano piece made out of that roll.

The surface of the ball is divided into tiles (like a globe): bands between the poles, and meridians dividing each band into seven slivers. Every tile is mapped to a scale degree. Moving one tile along a parallel is a step of the scale, moving one band toward the light pole is a step too (with one bigger leap hidden in the middle of the ball), and the heavy pole is do. The sensor doesn’t know where the ball is in the room, but it always knows which way is down, and that is all it needs to tell which tile is touching the floor. The tiles the ball rests on, in order, are the melody. Since the heavy pole is where every roll ends, every melody ends on the tonic. Gravity (the real one!) does the resolving.

Two more things come from the roll. How far the ball turned around the vertical, between the push and the rest, picks the mode: seven sectors of a full turn, from Lydian around to Locrian. And how many times it hit the wall picks the time signature: two hits is 2/4, three is 3/4, up to 9/8 before it loops back around. Some time signatures are played as meters characteristic of Bulgarian folk music. For example, 7/8 is played as long-short-short, and 8/8, as long-short-long.

Making the Ball

The ball is two printed hemispheres that press-fit together, with a Seeed XIAO nRF52840 Sense, a battery, and the weight inside. The sensor talks to the Pi over Bluetooth, so once the ball is closed, that is it: nothing can be plugged in, and everything about the ball, including which way its heavy pole points and where its band edges sit, has to be sent in over the air. That turned out to be a good constraint. It forced me to make everything about the ball tunable from a text file on the Pi, and the ball itself only ever reports tile indices; what they mean musically is decided elsewhere.

The press fit was the first thing to go wrong. The first print closed but didn’t stay closed (you don't want the sphere to open mid-roll). I reprinted with a tighter fit, which was then too tight to close easily, and reprinted again. The version that finally held took a couple of iterations of adjusting the lip by fractions of a millimeter.

The Custom Sensor: Hearing the Wall

Counting wall hits sounded like the easy part: two piezo discs glued under the arena’s support plates feel the ring vibrate when the ball hits it, and an ESP32 hidden under the arena counts the spikes. It was in fact the easy part, but it took longer than it should have.

The first problem was wires. The piezos came with thin, fragile leads, and the ESP32 sits under the arena, far from both discs. My first idea was copper tape running along the underside of the plywood: stick the leads to the tape, stick the tape to the board. Securing contact between the copper tape and the wire leads was very unreliable. I ended up clamping each piezo lead into a screw terminal right next to the disc and running proper, longer wires from the terminals to the ESP32.

The second problem: with the wires sorted, the piezos were detecting the wrong hits. A tap on the ring with a finger registered; a ball rolling into the wall at speed sometimes didn’t; the ball brushing the wall for half a second sometimes counted twice; someone bumping the table counted once. Thresholds didn’t fix it, because a hard knock on one side of the ring and a soft one on the other look the same to a disc that only feels how much the wood shakes.

The fix was to incorporate the accelerometer in the ball: when it hits the wall it feels a jolt of several g, which nothing else in a roll looks like. So the ball reports every knock it feels, with a timestamp, and the Pi counts a piezo hit only if the ball felt an impact within a tenth of a second of it. In other words, the piezos say that an impact was felt, and the ball confirms it was the one doing the hitting. This piezo-plus-IMU arrangement is the custom sensor in the project.

From a Roll to a Piece

What the Pi does with a roll is where the faking-art question is. The tiles become notes, but a ball produces notes with no sense of time, so the rhythm is made up: a measure-by-measure pattern machine that fills each beat with a cell (a quarter, two eighths, an eighth and two sixteenths…), repeats or varies the previous measure the way a phrase would, and lets the final note take the rest of its bar. Under the melody, a left hand picks chords. For every slot it considers every chord of the mode that contains the melody note and searches for the smoothest path between voicings, as a pianist’s hand often would (well, if they think about voice leading), starting and ending on the tonic in root position. Then FluidSynth plays it as a piano, with the pedal down under each harmony and the ending held and faded rather than cut.

The part that makes it legible to a person standing there, though, is what happens before the piece: while the ball is rolling, every tile it touches sounds immediately, with the pedal down for the whole roll. You hear the raw material, every wobble and double visit included, and then you hear the same notes come back a second later, cleaned up and set in time, in a mode you chose by how much you spun it. The transition from noise to music is the whole point, and it is audible.

Does it work? It does what my friend’s black keys did, with a lot more machinery: a scale to trust and a meter to stand on, and a resolution that is guaranteed by physics. I don’t think the pieces are good, but I think they are convincingly pieces. Good enough.

Can I Roll It Again?

Yes. The Pi starts the whole thing on boot, reconnects to the ball when it wakes up, and a new push fades out whatever is still playing.
