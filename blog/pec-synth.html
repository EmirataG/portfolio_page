Pec Synth / MPec / Absolute Absurdity

Prelude (or how my sick mind came up with this)

I love playing Dixit, but I don’t really like the game… I just like the art on the cards. I mean, just take a look at these:

Many adjectives could describe the examples above, but absurd is one that easily applies to all of them. This is the kind of absurdity whose presence for its own sake seems improbable, leaving the observer searching for a deeper meaning. If found, that meaning is often accompanied by doubt in the intention of the artist to convey it. That is, the meaning often turns out to be created rather than discovered.

Other times, absurdity in art is a vehicle for eliciting shock or surprise. A Day in the Life by The Beatles comes to mind: elements of absurdity can be found both in the composition and in the lyrics. Lennon’s main verses, McCartney’s upbeat middle section, and the orchestral transitions between the two feel like they’re coming from completely different musical worlds; on the first few listens, the contrast is genuinely jarring. Also what is John saying here? Laughing at a photograph of someone who “blew his mind out in a car” … Why?

What the Hell is This Device

Yes, I am really fascinated by absurdity, and recently I decided to turn my most absurd shower thought of late into a real project. The shower thought in question… “What if I could play Turkish maqams by flexing my chest muscles?” I had two spare Seeed XIAO nRF52840 Sense boards collecting dust in my room, so I figured that I could use their built-in accelerometers and gyroscopes to detect changes in pose induced by chest muscle flexion, so the vision was there: the two Sense boards wrapped in small enclosures and velcroed to my nipples bouncing up and down as I flex my chest and sending signals to an ESP 32 that are ultimately translated to pitches by a Raspberry Pi 5 wired to a USB speaker. Just imagine this GIF of The Rock except every bounce produces the next note in a traditional Ottoman tune.

My sincerest apologies if you feel uncomfortable reading this, but absurdity is a crucial part of the experience here.

To be more specific about the pec-to-music mapping:

  • Right pec = go up the scale (to the upper neighbor)
  • Left pec = go down the scale (to the lower neighbor)
  • Both pecs = repeat the current note

The Process

First I had to get the core of the firmware going—namely, how do I get the Sense boards to detect muscle flexion. The idea was simple: assuming that the user has good pectoral muscle control, flexing it should create a spike in the accelerometer and gyroscope readings. The first script I tested did not get the job done: it was too sensitive to noise, and classified almost every motion as both pecs being flexed (one could argue that this is a muscle control skill issue on my end.) So I knew I needed data—what separated change in pose due to noise and real flexion. I asked Claude to write a script that would record me doing a pre-defined set of movements at specified times. Running it produced a time series dataset where each row was stamped with “right pec flexed”, “left pec flexed”, “both pecs flexed”, and “idle”. With this information, Claude was able to derive good-enough heuristics, and flex detection became reliable.

Latency is a thing I kept coming back to. My first instinct was that the Bluetooth speaker was to blame, and it was partly right: PipeWire reported 141 ms of buffering on it. But rather than guess, Claude measured each stage separately. A single flex was waiting 200 ms just to see whether the other pec would join in; checking whether the other board was moving at all cut that to 20 ms. Deciding “left, right or both” 40 ms after a flex starts, instead of waiting for it to peak, took recognition from 108 ms to 43 with no loss of accuracy on the recording. When the USB speaker I’d ordered from Amazon finally arrived, it removed the Bluetooth delay; the instrument finally felt like it was listening to me rather than politely waiting for the next beat.

Then I could finally look at the artifact before me as a real instrument rather than a pipeline. I replaced the Western scales I used for testing with eight Turkish maqams on C, chosen so that none need quarter tones and all contain the G of the drone; every run starts on C, on a strong beat, so you always know where you are. I designed an enclosure with a button and two potentiometers, logarithmic ones, as I could find no linear ones in the makerspace, which the firmware has to un-warp so that equal turns mean equal changes. While you play, the scale knob sets how far a flex jumps, from repeating the same note up to a full octave, and the tempo knob bends the melody up to a quarter tone either way, with the drone staying put so you hear the detuning.
