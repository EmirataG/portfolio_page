Faking Art

The Vision

When my best friend from high school asked me how to fake piano skills, I told him to follow 2 simple steps:

  1. Holding the pedal down, play black notes in any order with the right hand.
  2. Play an Eb + Bb with the left hand at regular intervals.

He sat down and in a few minutes was playing what to a non-musician might sound like chill cocktail piano music. For sound to be perceived as music (I am just trying to make a point, not offer a definition for music; the latter is an ambitious undertaking) a sense of regularity in time and coherence in the choice of pitch classes is usually more than enough. In the “technique” my friend is now a master of, the open fifth in the left hand creates a sense of metric grounding, while the pentatonic scale makes it pretty much impossible for any note played to sound particularly unexpected or out of place. The applications of this simple idea are, fortunately, not confined to faking piano skills—while actual improvisation presupposes more musicality and intentionality, it still strongly relies on a sense of musical time and “trust” in the chosen scale.

This project started with the question of whether this idea had its analog in the world of visual art. That is, are there any two (or more, or less) principles so fundamental that, even paired with absolute randomness, can create visually appealing art if followed? Essentially, this project is a test of the hypothesis that these two principles are spatial bias and palette. The former is the analog of musical meter. Notes are laid out in time and a completely random layout that doesn’t fit in any metric grid is hardly ever appealing to the ear. (Static) Visual art is laid out in space and coherence tends not to arise when strokes and splashes are thrown around randomly. The equivalent of metric grounding here could be a point that all visual elements are biased towards. The palette is the scale—it ensures that no color sticks out in an unpleasant way. “How can I fake art skills” isn’t a question I feel qualified to answer, but this project explores whether the principles described above constitute a viable approach.

The Process, The Decisions

The initial vision for the project was as follows:

  1. The piece starts as a blank canvas, and some number of colors belonging to a pre-defined palette get spawned as circles at random locations.
  2. An attraction function uses distance between circles and their colors to calculate a direction vector for each at each time step.
  3. Based on the vector, each circle updates its location, leaving a solid trace behind.
  4. To introduce spatial bias, there is an invisible point that all colors are strongly attracted to (the gravity point.)
  5. Every time two circles collide, they spawn a new one at the point of collision with a color that is a mix of theirs and an additional one with a random color from the palette at a random point (so that the number of circles is always the same).
  6. We just sit back and watch art being made.

Some problems with this plan were caught upon first inspection, before any coding. In retrospect, the one that looks the most glaring is the mixing of colors—it essentially guarantees that the colors will start drifting away from the palette, completely undermining the hypothesis the project is built on. Dropping this idea proved very convenient too, as the attraction function’s job became significantly easier—since the number of colors that could appear on the canvas was now bounded, “attraction constants” could be stored in a matrix where entry [i][j] shows how much the color at index i of the color list attracts or repels the color at index j.

Getting colored circles to move around on the screen unveiled more issues, showing that I was still far from the optimal setup to test my hypothesis. First, having new circles spawn on collision points turned out to have a pretty underwhelming effect: it was often the case that collisions happened very close to other circles headed to the same point. The newly spawned circle would therefore immediately find another one to collide with, often barely leaving a trace. The simplest fix was to have both new circles spawn at random locations, and while it was a downgrade in complexity, it produced more appealing results. One thing to note is that, in the very first version, I hadn’t implemented the gravity point, and while it was somewhat fun to see the colors move and change directions as the scene progressed, the output looked and felt like nothing more than randomness.

A major problem in the setup was that the artwork was never “done”: colors just kept spawning. Fortunately, this had an easy solution: do not let colors cross existing traces and stop when no more circles can be spawned. Beyond fixing the issue, implementing this had an unexpected positive side effect: before, despite the attraction relationships, colors would generally go in the direction of the gravity point in a fairly predictable way. Now, traces acted as obstacles and made trajectories a lot more varied and interesting—randomness seemed to be contributing in a beautiful way. However, an artifact of the inherent randomness that I really disliked was the occasional-but-frequent-enough clustering of traces of the same color. To get rid of it, I made the diagonal entries in the attraction matrix negative and large in magnitude so that instances of the same color strongly repel each other.

In terms of implementation, not crossing a trace comes down to looking before stepping. Each frame, a circle probes three points just past its leading edge—the center and the two corners of the stroke—and checks whether the paint layer already has color there. If the way is clear it moves; if not, it tries the same step rotated by 30°, then 60°, then 90° to either side, which is why traces slide along each other instead of stopping dead on contact. Only when every direction is blocked does the circle give up, and a new one is spawned somewhere blank. 

// splash.js (simplified)
pathClear(v) {
  const ahead = v.copy().setMag(this.r + 2);
  const side = createVector(-ahead.y, ahead.x).setMag(this.r - 1);
  for (const p of [ahead, ahead.copy().add(side), ahead.copy().sub(side)]) {
    if (isPainted(this.pos.x + p.x, this.pos.y + p.y)) return false;
  }
  return true;
}

move() {
  for (const angle of DEFLECT_ANGLES) { // 0, ±30°, ±60°, ±90°
    const v = this.vel.copy().rotate(angle);
    if (!this.pathClear(v)) continue;
    this.vel = v;
    this.pos.add(this.vel);
    return;
  }
  this.blocked = true; // nowhere to go: replaced next frame
}

The last problem I had to address was all the blank space that remained after no circle could be spawned. One idea I had was to keep making the circle radii smaller, allowing colors to be spawned in tighter spaces, until everything was colored, but I ended up implementing something conceptually simpler: each trace starts expanding in every direction until it meets another one or hits a wall. This introduced a variety of shapes in the final result and made it look a lot more like an actual painting. And… I was pretty happy with what I was seeing! More importantly, I believe that my hypothesis, while by no stretch of my imagination groundbreaking, at least seemed to be confirmed: the output of each run of the generation process looked like something created with intentionality.

There are a few things to say about the choice of colors too. Initially, I hadn’t chosen a palette. I was rolling with a set of colors that Claude suggested but soon enough realized that my AI assistant, as impressive as it is, didn’t quite have the artistic taste of its French namesake. I tried to think of paintings whose palettes meaningfully capture something in their choice of color alone. The Harvester by the celebrated Bulgarian painter Vladimir “the Master” Dimitrov does it for me—I cross my eyes to blur my vision, and even without making out the subject, I see, feel, and smell the village, the sun, and the crushingly hard work. So I took 15 of the colors used in it and made them my palette.

A less-deep-but-deeply-effective creative choice I made along the way was to replace the white background with a zoomed-in image of a piece of paper and make the circles and traces translucent. This creates a sense of realism that, I believe, really adds to the appeal of the generated artwork. Simply lowering the opacity of the strokes didn’t work, though: a trace is drawn as many overlapping short segments, so translucent color piles up and goes solid. Instead, the circles paint opaque colors into an offscreen layer, and every frame the paper image is multiplied over it, so the texture shows through the colors evenly. 

// splash.js: each step is drawn into the offscreen `paint` layer
show() {
  paint.stroke(PALETTE_HEX[this.col_id]);
  paint.strokeWeight(this.r * 2);
  paint.line(this.prev.x, this.prev.y, this.pos.x, this.pos.y);
}
// sketch.js: screen = paint x paper
function composite() {
  image(paint, 0, 0, width, height);
  blendMode(MULTIPLY);
  drawPaper();
  blendMode(BLEND);
}

The Important Question: Can I Plug This into Any Monitor?

Yes, as far as my testing could prove. The radii of the circles (and therefore the width of the traces) and their speed are a function of the window size. Bigger screen = wider traces and faster circles; smaller screen = thinner traces and slower circles. This ensures that just a few strokes can never fill up the whole canvas and that the canvas never takes too long to fill up, even if it is very big.
